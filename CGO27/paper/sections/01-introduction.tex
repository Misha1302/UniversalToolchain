\section{Introduction}
Extensible compilers are assembled from language frontends, optimization modules, intermediate-representation (IR) utilities, and backends that may have been developed independently. Their shared IR normally has a structural verifier: opcodes must be legal, operands must have the expected shape, branches must target existing blocks, and stack effects must balance. Those checks are necessary, but they do not capture every relation introduced by a selected compiler configuration. A structurally legal optimizer can invalidate a language-specific shape fact, a producer/source identity, or a backend-capability assumption without making the generic IR malformed.

Analysis invalidation addresses a related but narrower problem. A pass manager can discard cached analyses and recompute one when a later pass requests it. That prevents a consumer from reading a stale cached result. It does not force any consumer to make the request, nor does it express that a semantic fact must be re-established before an artifact crosses a particular compiler boundary. Generic verification has the complementary limitation: it can reject malformed IR, but not a selected-system relation that is absent from its schema.

We study \emph{obligation-guided reverification}. Transformations declare typed effect contracts over semantic facts. Invalidating a fact creates a named verification obligation carrying its canonical verifier owner, creation boundary, and first eligible boundary. The scheduler must execute every due obligation through the unique canonical route, or fail closed before the artifact crosses that boundary. This turns ``an analysis became stale'' into ``this selected-system fact must be re-established here.''

Our guarantee is intentionally relative. If the fact vocabulary and transformation effects are complete for the considered relation, the route is unique and executable, the scheduler runs at every modeled boundary, and the verifier is sound for its fact, then selective policy P2 cannot let an artifact cross its first eligible boundary with a due false or undischarged considered fact. The guarantee does not prove effect completeness, verifier correctness, or whole-compiler correctness.

We compare five policies over the same production verifiers: structural-only P0; passive invalidation P1; demand-driven recomputation P1D; obligation-guided selective verification P2; and always verification P3. The strongest competing baseline, P1D, safely recomputes an invalidated fact when it is explicitly queried. A matched counterexample isolates the difference: with no downstream query, P1D leaves a false fact pending, while P2 rejects at the declared semantic boundary.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a model of owned, boundary-indexed verification obligations over explicit compiler-fact effects;
  \item a deterministic fail-closed selective scheduler and a conditional guarantee proved by induction over pipeline boundaries;
  \item an implementation in a composed .NET compiler and its public extension boundary, including an executable demand-recomputation baseline;
  \item versioned boundary and source-to-result evaluations with matched controls, mechanism ablations, and a separately reported historical/external-validity track.
\end{itemize}

The empirical results are corpus-bounded. In the versioned boundary study, P1D detects the queried demand case but misses its otherwise identical unqueried counterpart; P2/P3 detect both. In 32 fresh-process source programs, P2/P3 agree on classification and first boundary for all cases, including seven targeted faults, while P1D catches only the explicitly queried demand fault. These results support an enforcement and localization claim, not universal P2/P3 equivalence or a whole-compilation speedup.
