\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Pass managers and analysis invalidation.}
LLVM's new pass manager caches analyses and uses preservation sets to decide which cached results remain valid after a transformation~\cite{llvmnpm}.  Analyses may implement transitive invalidation through their dependencies; passes may also update selected analyses manually.  MLIR similarly computes analyses lazily, assumes them invalidated after a pass unless explicitly preserved, and lets an analysis inspect preservation of its dependencies~\cite{mlirpm}.  These mechanisms answer whether a cached analysis result may be reused.  P1D is deliberately the strongest direct analogue in our study: an invalidated fact is recomputed when queried.  Our additional requirement is temporal and ownership-bearing: invalidation creates an obligation that must be discharged by one canonical production route no later than the first declared semantic boundary, even if no downstream consumer requests the fact.  Thus the comparison is not ``invalidation versus no invalidation,'' but consumer-triggered availability versus boundary-triggered enforcement.

\paragraph{Translation validation.}
Translation validation checks each produced translation rather than proving the implementation of every transformation~\cite{pnueli98,necula00}.  Formally verified validators can move trust from an optimizer to a smaller checker for a particular transformation family~\cite{tristan08}.  Alive and Alive2 automate validation for LLVM rewrites and LLVM IR, respectively~\cite{alive,alive2}; Alive2 is bounded and can return inconclusive outcomes when its resource bounds are insufficient.  These systems define strong semantic relations for particular IRs or transformations.  We address a complementary scheduling problem: an extensible pipeline already contains heterogeneous structural, source-identity, layout, capability, and backend verifiers, but composition must determine which selected relation became suspect, who owns its checker, and where the check becomes mandatory.  An obligation route may invoke a translation validator, yet the scheduler neither replaces nor strengthens that validator's semantics.

\paragraph{Verified, typed, and certifying compilation.}
CompCert proves semantic preservation for a substantial compiler stack~\cite{leroy06,leroy09backend}.  Typed assembly languages preserve machine-checkable typing invariants across lowering~\cite{tal}; proof-carrying code and certifying compilers separate evidence production from a trusted consumer-side checker~\cite{pcc,certcompiler}.  These approaches provide stronger assurance than our conditional theorem.  Obligation-guided reverification assumes effect completeness and verifier soundness, and it does not emit a proof of whole-program equivalence.  Its narrower goal is to make existing production checks unavoidable at selected composition boundaries without requiring every extension to participate in one proof development.

\paragraph{Compiler testing and reduction.}
Differential, random, and metamorphic systems such as Csmith, EMI, and YARPGen discover wrong-code and crash-inducing programs~\cite{csmith,emi,yarpgen}.  C-Reduce, Perses, and delta debugging make failures smaller and reproducible~\cite{creduce,perses,delta}.  Our evaluation uses targeted faults and controls to test a scheduling mechanism; it does not propose a new generator or claim natural defect prevalence.  The distinction matters for interpretation: a detected study fault is evidence that a declared obligation reaches its verifier, not evidence that the mechanism discovers more unknown compiler bugs than fuzzing.  Conversely, fuzzing can supply future historical or externally authored cases, but it does not by itself enforce that a selected semantic relation is checked before a backend consumes an artifact.

The table exposes the precise novelty boundary. Pass managers already provide selective invalidation and lazy recomputation, while translation validators and verified compilers provide stronger semantic checkers or proofs. Our contribution is neither a new semantic relation nor a replacement for those systems. It is the composition contract that turns a selected-system invalidation into a uniquely owned, boundary-deadlined execution obligation and makes failure to resolve that route a compilation error. The source-to-result gate is consequently a property of the scheduler integration: the same production verifier that may be optional or consumer-triggered elsewhere becomes mandatory for a due fact before backend consumption.

\paragraph{Positioning.}
The closest material alternative is a composition of established techniques: lazy pass-manager invalidation, production semantic validators, and aggressive compiler testing.  That combination is attractive and often sufficient.  The matched queried/unqueried case identifies its remaining gap: a known-invalid semantic fact can cross a meaningful boundary when no consumer asks for it.  P2 adds a typed deadline and canonical owner to close exactly that gap.  Its benefit is therefore conditional on a pipeline having meaningful selected-system relations and executable verifiers; for a monolithic compiler with one always-run validator, or for a fact whose first use always queries its analysis, P2 may add little beyond existing practice.
