\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}
\paragraph{Constructed corpora and author/model involvement.}
The primary, challenge, demand, source-to-result, and Language T faults were designed during the study and may align with implementation structure. Repetitions test reproducibility, not independent semantic sampling. We report paired case counts and all misses; we do not infer prevalence or generality. A human-authored frozen corpus remains \texttt{BLOCKED\_EXTERNAL}.

\paragraph{Historical selection and replay.}
A historical corpus can reduce direct construction bias, but author-selected inclusion introduces its own bias. We froze criteria before search, retained exclusions and blocked aggregate fixes, and derived the three included oracles from original issue reports rather than P2 output. The exact pre-fix revision reproduces all three cases in all nine fresh-process attempts. This establishes reproducibility, not policy effectiveness: the revision predates P2, no backport was run, and the cases are not folded into P2 detection totals or called independent.

\paragraph{Seed and effect trust.}
The theorem is conditional. An unsound initial seed, false production or preservation declaration, omitted fact, or missing invalidation can make the runtime contract state disagree with semantic truth. Fail-closed missing-route handling protects only facts declared invalidated; the scheduler cannot discover an unmodeled relation or prove extension metadata. The evaluated vocabulary is deliberately finite and cannot justify a whole-compiler correctness claim.

\paragraph{Verifier soundness and shared implementation.}
A uniquely owned route can still contain a bug. P2 and P3 share verifier implementations, so their parity cannot validate verifier soundness. The study tests selected negative and control cases but does not prove the verifier.  System W and Language T also share scheduler code, so the second package tests public extensibility and a different fact vocabulary, not an independent scheduler implementation.

\paragraph{Baseline choice.}
P1D is a realistic lazy-recomputation baseline, but implementations differ in which uses trigger queries and whether passes eagerly update analyses. The unqueried counterexample establishes a separation between two guarantees; it does not claim that LLVM, MLIR, or every demand-driven compiler accepts the concrete study fault. MLIR-style verification after every pass is stronger than P1D and may already close the gap when one available verifier covers the relevant relation. P3 runs every registered route at each represented protocol boundary, not necessarily after every individual pass, and the study provides no whole-compilation comparison with a per-pass verifier.

\paragraph{Performance.}
Verifier-work counts and boundary-kernel timing do not establish whole-compilation performance. Shared hosted runners are not pinned. Until the machine protocol runs, overhead and speedup remain unknown and absent from the headline claims.  The observed 25\% control-call reduction meets the isolated-work threshold, but does not establish whole-compilation speedup.

\paragraph{Generative-AI involvement.}
Generative AI assisted text, code, workflow orchestration, analysis, and adversarial review. Model-generated tests and reviews are not independent evidence. The disclosure records this scope, and the submission remains contingent on human inspection of final diffs, logs, sources, tables, and artifacts.

\paragraph{Anonymity.}
The public project, development branch, manuscript title, and intermediate result summaries have appeared in public history. Rewriting that history would be misleading and would not reliably remove mirrors or caches. Sanitizing the final PDF and package prevents direct identity leakage but cannot eliminate motivated search-based deanonymization. The final title and submission metadata should therefore be kept out of further public development records, and the program chairs should be asked whether the existing exposure requires any additional handling.
